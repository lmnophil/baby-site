Crying and soothing

Crying is a newborn’s way to ask for food, comfort, warmth, sleep, or help. Responding promptly will not spoil a young baby, and sometimes a well baby keeps crying after every need has been checked.

Check illness first. Get prompt medical advice for a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby younger than 3 months. Call 911 for trouble breathing, blue or gray color, a seizure, or a baby who is limp or unresponsive.

What crying may mean right now

In the first days, needs can arrive close together. Make one calm pass through the basics:

  1. Look for hunger. Offer a feed when the baby shows their usual cues, following the plan from the birth or pediatric care team.
  2. Check comfort. Change a wet or soiled diaper; feel the chest or back for heat or chill; loosen tight clothing; and look for a hair around a finger or toe.
  3. Lower the stimulation. Dim the room and try gentle rocking, walking, skin-to-skin contact, a supported cuddle, or a quiet voice. Burping after a feed may also help.

The AAP recommends responding promptly while caregivers learn their baby’s patterns; its crying guide offers more soothing options.

If soothing turns into sleep: place the baby on their back in a crib, bassinet, portable crib, or play yard with a firm, flat mattress and fitted sheet—no pillows, loose blankets, toys, or other soft objects. Move a sleeping baby out of a swing, carrier, stroller, or car seat when not traveling. These are the AAP safe-sleep basics.

What may change next

  • Over the next couple of weeks: crying may become louder, more recognizable, and more frequent. The NHS notes that crying often increases at about 2 weeks and may cluster in the afternoon or evening.
  • Around 4–6 weeks: many babies reach their fussiest stretch. A baby may be hard to settle for a time yet feed, grow, and act content between episodes.
  • After about 2 months: crying often begins to ease; it is commonly much better by 3 months, though some babies take closer to 4 months, according to the AAP crying symptom guide.

These ages are guides, not deadlines. Watch for longer calm or alert periods, more predictable triggers, and more reliable responses to familiar comfort.

What looks reassuring

Look for a cluster of signs rather than expecting every soothing attempt to work:

  • The baby has their usual color, breathing, movement, and alertness between cries.
  • They feed regularly and have wet diapers following the expected newborn trend.
  • They settle at least some of the time with feeding, holding, sucking, or sleep.
  • There are calm periods, and the overall pattern is not suddenly worsening.

When to call or act

Call the pediatrician

  • Crying repeatedly happens during or after feeds, or feeding seems difficult.
  • The baby seems hungry after most feeds, is not gaining as expected, or the usual wet-diaper pattern drops.
  • A new crying pattern continues, even if the baby can still be comforted.
  • The crying is wearing the caregiver down or raising any concern.

Get prompt medical advice

  • A rectal temperature is 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months.
  • A baby under 1 month looks or acts different from usual in any way.
  • Crying is nonstop for more than 2 hours and the baby cannot be consoled.
  • The baby drinks very little, vomits more than ordinary spit-up, or cries when touched or moved.
  • The cry sounds unusual and the baby also seems unwell, unusually sleepy, or less responsive.

These thresholds come from the AAP’s crying symptom guide for young babies. Do not give fever medicine to a young infant before a clinician advises it.

Get urgent help now

Call 911 or local emergency services if the baby:

  • struggles to breathe, grunts, or pulls in under the ribs;
  • has blue or gray lips, tongue, or skin;
  • has a seizure; or
  • is limp, unresponsive, very weak, or will not wake normally.

These signs need emergency care. See the AAP’s breathing-trouble symptom guide for more detail.

Usually not a worry by itself

  • Passing gas or making stomach noises while crying when the baby is well between episodes.
  • A fussy time that tends to arrive in the afternoon or evening.
  • One soothing method working yesterday but not today.
  • Crying with no obvious cause when the baby is well between episodes.

These patterns deserve medical advice when they arrive with a fever, poor feeding, fewer wet diapers, vomiting, breathing trouble, unusual sleepiness, pain with touch or movement, or a baby who otherwise seems unwell.

When the caregiver needs a reset

Feeling overwhelmed is a cue to make the situation safer. Put the baby on their back in an empty safe-sleep space, step away, and ask another calm adult to take over if possible. Check back every 5–10 minutes. Never shake, throw, hit, or jerk a baby.

The CDC explains why crying can trigger abusive head trauma and recommends a safe break and support. If anyone may hurt the baby, put the baby somewhere safe, call a trusted person who can come now, and call emergency services if the baby cannot be kept safe.

Useful today

  • Save a short soothing sequence: feed or check cues, change, cuddle, lower stimulation, then reassess.
  • Keep the safe-sleep space clear and ready before a tired caregiver needs a break.
  • Note when crying starts, how it relates to feeds and sleep, what helps, and any illness signs to share with the pediatrician.
  • Choose a backup person now and agree that either caregiver can hand off without judgment.
