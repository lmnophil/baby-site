Newborn skin

Peeling, tiny white bumps, blotches, and shifting color are common in the first days and weeks. Most need time and gentle care—not a product—but new blisters, increasing yellow color, or a baby who seems unwell should not be treated as an ordinary newborn rash.

What you may see right now

Newborn skin is adjusting from the womb to air, clothing, moisture, and temperature. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guide to newborn appearance describes several normal changes:

  • Dryness and peeling: flakes often show most around the wrists, ankles, hands, and feet. Peeling can happen whether a baby was born early, on time, or late and usually needs no treatment.
  • Milia: pinhead-size white or yellow bumps can sit across the nose, cheeks, or chin. They are not infected pimples.
  • Erythema toxicum: red blotches with a small white or yellow bump in the middle may appear during the first few days, fade in one spot, and show up in another.
  • Temporary color shifts: skin may look mottled or blotchy when baby is cool and return toward its usual color after warming. Hands and feet can also look mildly blue or purple when cold.
  • Birthmarks: flat pink patches on the eyelids or neck and flat blue-gray areas, often on the back or buttocks, are common. Their visibility varies across skin tones.

Check yellow color in good light

Jaundice is yellow color caused by bilirubin in the blood. It often starts on the face during the first days and can be harder to see on darker skin; look at the whites of the eyes and gums too. Keep every scheduled bilirubin and newborn follow-up check. The AAP’s newborn jaundice guidance explains when yellow color needs a call.

What changes next

  • Over the next few days: leftover vernix—the creamy white coating from birth—absorbs or washes away. Peeling may become more noticeable. Do not pull the flakes.
  • During the first week or two: erythema toxicum can come and go before clearing. Mild birth-related bruises and scrapes should also become less noticeable, not redder, wetter, or more tender.
  • Over the coming weeks: milia and fine body hair usually fade on their own. Some flat pink marks lighten more slowly, while a raised red birthmark may first become noticeable. These are broad patterns, not deadlines.

Reassuring change means the overall area is settling: fewer new spots, less redness, intact skin underneath flakes, and no change in feeding, alertness, breathing, or comfort.

Reassuring signs

  • Peeling skin is dry underneath, without cracks, bleeding, swelling, or drainage.
  • Blotches come and go while baby otherwise wakes, feeds, and acts as usual.
  • Mottling or mildly blue hands and feet improves after baby is comfortably warmed.
  • Skin folds are clean and dry, without spreading redness or a strong odor.
  • A mark present from birth stays the same shape or begins to fade; a photo can help you compare it over time.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • A mild rash does not match a familiar pattern, keeps spreading, or is not beginning to settle.
  • Dry skin becomes cracked, raw, itchy-looking, or does not improve with less bathing and fragrance-free moisturizer.
  • A birthmark is newly raised or changing; ask the clinician to identify and document it.

Get prompt medical advice

  • There are fluid-filled blisters, clustered sores, pus, yellow crust, spreading redness, warmth, swelling, or pain. Even harmless newborn conditions can look blister-like, so the AAP advises having blister-like rashes evaluated.
  • Yellow color is increasing, reaches the belly, arms, or legs, or appears in the whites of the eyes.
  • Baby is hard to wake, unusually fussy, or not nursing or taking a bottle well—especially with yellow skin.

Get urgent help now

A rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby 3 months or younger needs an immediate call to the pediatrician, even if there are no other symptoms. See the AAP’s infant fever guidance.

Seek emergency medical care if the lips, tongue, face, or body stay blue or gray after baby is calm and warm, or if baby is struggling to breathe. Persistently blue central skin needs immediate medical attention, according to the AAP’s guide to common newborn conditions.

Usually not a worry

  • Peeling without discomfort: do not scrub, exfoliate, or pick. Intact skin underneath is reassuring.
  • Milia: leave the tiny white bumps alone; squeezing or acne medicine can irritate newborn skin.
  • A dramatic-looking blotchy rash: erythema toxicum can be widespread and still harmless when baby is well. If you are not sure that is what you see, call.
  • Cool-weather mottling: blotchy color that clears with gentle warming is common. Blue or gray lips, tongue, face, or body that persists is different and needs urgent help.

Useful today

  1. Look at baby’s skin in daylight: note whether yellow color is increasing and whether any redness is spreading.
  2. Clean milk, spit-up, urine, and stool from skin folds with lukewarm water, then pat dry rather than rubbing.
  3. Keep bathing brief and infrequent. Use cleanser only where needed, and choose a mild product without fragrance.
  4. If skin is dry, apply a small amount of fragrance-free moisturizer; the AAP’s bathing and skin-care guidance recommends gentle washing and notes that frequent baths can worsen dryness.
  5. Take a clear photo of a new or changing mark in the same light so the pediatrician can see the trend.
