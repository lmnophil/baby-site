Formula preparation and storage

Follow the directions on the specific formula container exactly, measure water first when mixing is required, and start clean. Powdered formula is not sterile, so babies younger than 2 months—and babies born prematurely or with a weakened immune system—need extra precautions.

Full guide

The dated summary changes with the card you choose. The complete reference below stays available for context and safety.

A safe bottle right now

First, check which kind of formula you have. The preparation is not interchangeable:

  • Ready-to-feed liquid: serve it exactly as the label directs; do not add water.
  • Liquid concentrate: add the exact amount of safe water stated on its label.
  • Powder: measure the label’s exact amount of safe water into the bottle first, then add powder using the scoop supplied with that container.

Too much water can dangerously upset a young baby’s mineral balance; too little can cause dehydration. Do not stretch formula, make homemade formula, or use a generic scoop ratio. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) formula-mixing guide explains why the container directions matter.

For a baby at higher risk

For a baby younger than 2 months, born prematurely, or with a weakened immune system, powdered formula needs extra precautions because it is not sterile. If using powder, boil safe water, let it cool for about 5 minutes, and mix while the water is still very hot. Then cool the capped bottle before feeding. Very hot water helps kill germs such as Cronobacter that may be in powder.

Follow the CDC’s current formula preparation instructions. A specialty metabolic formula may have different temperature directions to protect its nutrients; its label and the baby’s clinician take priority.

  1. Check the container: do not use formula past its “Use By” date, from a recalled lot, or from a damaged container. Stop if its smell or appearance is unexpected.
  2. Get clean: wash hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds. Clean the preparation surface and use a clean bottle; sanitize feeding items daily when baby is under 2 months or has the other higher-risk conditions.
  3. Measure and mix: follow the label exactly. Keep the scoop, lid, and powder completely dry; cap the bottle and shake rather than stirring with another utensil.
  4. Cool safely: formula does not need warming. After hot-water preparation, cool the capped bottle under cool running water or in an ice bath without wetting the nipple. Test a few drops on a wrist; they should feel warm, not hot.

The clocks that change next

  • When the bottle is prepared: use it within 2 hours. If feeding will not start soon, refrigerate it immediately and use it within 24 hours.
  • When feeding begins: a shorter clock takes over. Use the bottle within 1 hour from the start of the feed, then discard what remains; saliva allows bacteria to grow. The earliest limit always wins.
  • When a liquid container is opened: cover and refrigerate unused ready-to-feed or liquid concentrate. Follow its label; the AAP says opened liquid is generally discarded after 48 hours.
  • When a powder container is opened: write the date on the lid, close it tightly, and keep it in a cool, dry indoor place—not the refrigerator. Most powder must be used within 1 month, but check the label.
  • At and after 2 months: carefully clean feeding items after every use. Daily sanitizing may no longer be needed for a healthy baby, but prematurity or immune concerns keep the extra precautions active. The formula label, safe-water guidance, and storage clocks do not change.

The CDC preparation and storage guide and FDA infant formula safety guide support these preparation and storage limits.

Reassuring signs

A reliable routine matters more than whether a bottle looks “perfect”:

  • the formula form and label directions were checked before mixing;
  • the exact water and formula amounts were used, with water measured first;
  • bottle parts are fully taken apart, washed after every feed, air-dried completely, and stored in a clean place;
  • prepared and opened containers are dated or timed, refrigerated promptly when needed, and discarded at the correct limit; and
  • baby feeds with their usual alertness and continues the expected wet-diaper and follow-up weight pattern.

Contamination cannot always be seen or smelled, so a normal-looking bottle does not replace these steps. For a baby younger than 2 months, born prematurely, or with a weakened immune system, the CDC recommends sanitizing feeding items at least daily after cleaning. A dishwasher using hot water and heated drying or a sanitizing setting can complete both steps.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • You are unsure which formula instructions apply, whether local tap water is safe, or how to prepare a specialty formula.
  • You need a practical plan for safe formula access, travel, overnight feeds, or refrigeration.

Get prompt medical advice

  • Baby drank formula mixed with the wrong amount of water or powder. Do not try to balance it by giving plain water or an extra-strong bottle.
  • Baby may have had recalled, expired, damaged, improperly stored, or long-leftover formula.
  • Baby is feeding much less than usual, repeatedly vomiting, unusually sleepy, or making fewer wet diapers.

Get urgent help now

Cronobacter infection is rare, but it can be life-threatening in newborns. The CDC says it often begins with fever plus poor feeding, excessive crying, or very low energy. Take a baby with these signs to a medical provider as soon as possible. Call emergency services for a seizure or if baby is unresponsive.

See the CDC’s signs and symptoms of Cronobacter infection.

Usually not a worry

  • Serving formula cool or at room temperature: warming is optional if baby accepts it. Never microwave a bottle because hot spots can burn.
  • Using safe tap water: ordinary bottled water is not necessarily sterile. Safe tap water can be used as directed; keep the hot-water precaution for higher-risk babies.
  • Different directions between products: powder, concentrate, ready-to-feed, and specialty formulas are prepared differently. Rechecking the current label is safer than relying on memory.
  • Discarding unfinished formula: it can feel wasteful, but milk left after a feed should not be saved, even in the refrigerator. Preparing a smaller starting amount can reduce waste.

Useful today

  1. Put the storage rule where every caregiver can see it: 2 hours after mixing, 1 hour after feeding starts, 24 hours refrigerated.
  2. Write the opening date on powder and liquid containers, and check each label for its discard date.
  3. Use a separate basin when washing bottle parts by hand; do not wash them directly in the sink. Air-dry every part completely.
  4. If using powder for a higher-risk baby, practice the boil, 5-minute wait, hot mix, and safe-cooling sequence before the next feed.
