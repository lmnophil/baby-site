Clothing and room temperature

For sleep, dress baby in no more than one layer beyond what you would wear in the same room. Keep the head uncovered and use fitted sleep clothing or a correctly sized wearable blanket instead of loose bedding; sweating, a hot chest, or flushed skin mean remove a layer.

What works in the first weeks

In the early weeks, hands and feet may feel cooler or look bluer than the rest of the body because newborn circulation is still adjusting. That sign alone does not mean baby needs more bundling. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guide to the first month explains these early circulation and temperature changes.

A quick comfort check

  • Start simple: if the room feels comfortable to you, add at most one light layer for baby.
  • Check for overheating: sweating, a hot chest, or flushed skin means remove a layer.
  • Adjust one layer at a time: use light layers that are easy to add or remove when the room, weather, or activity changes.
  • Change damp clothing: after spit-up, a diaper leak, or sweating, replace wet fabric with something dry.

The AAP uses a comfortable room and baby’s signs rather than naming one required thermostat number. Its AAP safe-sleep guidance recommends no more than one extra layer, avoiding indoor hats once you are home from the hospital, and preventing overheating.

  • For every sleep: place baby on their back in a bare crib or bassinet. Use fitted pajamas or a well-fitting wearable blanket if another layer is needed.
  • Keep the face and head clear: skip indoor hats and keep loose blankets, quilts, pillows, and other soft objects out of the sleep space.
  • Never use weighted sleepwear: weighted blankets, swaddles, and sleep sacks are not safe for infants.
  • In hot weather: use fewer, loose, lightweight, light-colored layers. Never leave baby in a parked car, even briefly; cars can heat up dangerously fast.

What to reassess next

  1. Once home from the hospital: an indoor hat is no longer part of routine clothing. Follow the hospital or neonatal team’s plan if they gave different instructions.
  2. Whenever the setting changes: recheck the chest after moving indoors, entering a heated car, finishing skin-to-skin time, or changing the thermostat. The change in surroundings—not the clock—is the cue.
  3. As baby grows: check that pajamas and any wearable blanket still fit as directed and cannot cover the head. Follow the product’s stated size and weight limits.
  4. At any sign of trying to roll: stop swaddling and stop using any sleep sack that compresses the arms, chest, or body. A non-swaddling wearable blanket that allows free movement can continue. See the AAP’s swaddling transition guidance.

Reassuring signs

Look for a cluster of ordinary signs rather than trying to make every body part feel the same:

  • Baby’s chest feels comfortably warm and dry, without sweating or damp hair.
  • Breathing, skin color, feeding, waking, and movement look usual for that baby.
  • Clothes are dry, fit without tight marks, and stay clear of the nose and mouth.
  • At sleep, the head remains uncovered and the sleep space contains only baby and a fitted sheet.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

Ask during office hours if you are unsure how to dress baby for a setting, baby repeatedly seems uncomfortable despite simple layer changes, or the birth team gave an individual warmth plan because of prematurity, low birth weight, or illness.

Get prompt medical advice

If baby is younger than 3 months and has a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, call the pediatrician immediately—even if baby otherwise seems well. Feeling skin cannot confirm a fever; use a thermometer. The AAP infant fever guide explains the threshold and response. See also Fever and taking a temperature.

Get urgent help now

  • After cold exposure, bright red cold skin with very low energy can signal hypothermia. Move baby to warmth, remove wet clothes, and seek immediate medical attention; the CDC describes these infant hypothermia signs.
  • If you think baby has heatstroke after an overheated room, car, or hot weather, call 911 or go to an emergency department. The AAP explains why heatstroke needs emergency care.

Usually not a worry

  • Cool hands or feet: this is common in the early weeks. If baby’s chest is warm, lips and tongue are pink, and baby otherwise looks usual, extra bundling is not needed.
  • A bare-looking sleep space: a fitted sheet plus suitable sleep clothing is safer than adding a loose blanket. Bare does not mean baby is underdressed.
  • Using a different layer count from another baby: fabrics and rooms vary. Start with the one-extra-layer limit and watch for overheating signs instead of copying an outfit chart exactly.
  • Brief fussing while being changed: work one limb at a time and finish promptly. Fussing alone does not show that baby is too cold; use the overall comfort and safety checks instead of an outfit chart.

Useful today

  1. Set the room where baby sleeps to a temperature that feels comfortable to you.
  2. Choose a simple outfit with no more than one extra light layer, then remove a layer if baby is sweaty, flushed, or has a hot chest.
  3. Remove indoor hats and keep hoods, cords, loose blankets, and weighted products out of the sleep space.
  4. Check that pajamas and any wearable blanket fit now and cannot cover the face.
  5. Keep a digital thermometer easy to find, and share the 100.4°F (38°C) rule with every caregiver.
