Feeding

Breastfeeding and latch

A useful latch is deep enough for baby to remove milk and comfortable enough to continue: expect firm tugging, not ongoing pinching or pain. In the first days, both baby and caregiver are learning, so get hands-on help early if feeding hurts or milk transfer is unclear.

What a feeding can look like right now

Newborns commonly breastfeed 8–12 times in 24 hours and may ask as often as every 1–3 hours in the first days. Some feeds bunch together. Frequent feeding gives baby practice and helps build milk production; the CDC’s guide to early breastfeeding frequency explains how the pattern changes over time.

Bring baby’s chest toward your body with the head and body in one line. Start with the nose near the nipple, wait for a very wide mouth, then bring baby close so the chin meets the breast and the mouth takes in a generous amount of breast—not only the nipple. Different holds can work; comfort and active drinking matter more than a particular pose.

A quick latch check

  • Baby’s mouth is wide, lips turn outward, chin touches the breast, and the head does not have to turn to drink.
  • Sucking becomes deep and rhythmic, with pauses when baby swallows. Early colostrum swallows can be very quiet.
  • The latch feels comfortable; there is no pinching through the feed.
  • Afterward, the nipple looks much as it did before—not flattened or compressed.

These signs come from the HHS Office on Women’s Health latch guide.

What changes next

  • During the first day or two: baby takes small amounts of colostrum. Feeds may be brief or disorganized, and swallowing may be hard to hear. The reassuring transition is toward more wakeful, rhythmic sucking across repeated feeds—not one perfect session.
  • Around days 3–5: milk usually becomes more abundant. Breasts may feel fuller, and swallowing often becomes easier to see or hear. Very firm fullness can flatten the area around the nipple and make latching harder; ask for help if baby cannot attach.
  • Over the first 4–6 weeks: many babies become more efficient, so feeds may shorten and some gaps may lengthen. Breasts may feel softer and leak less as production adjusts. Those changes alone do not show low supply; diaper and weight trends are better checks.

The HHS timeline for making breast milk describes these broad transitions. They are guides, not deadlines.

Reassuring signs

Look for a cluster of signs across the day rather than judging one feed:

  • Baby latches, stays attached, and shifts from quick starting sucks to deeper sucks with pauses and swallows.
  • Hands and shoulders relax during at least some feeds, and baby releases the breast or seems settled afterward.
  • Feeds are comfortable overall, nipples remain intact, and breasts often feel softer after active drinking.
  • Wet and dirty diapers increase over the first week, and urine becomes pale rather than dark.
  • Weight begins trending upward at follow-up checks. A weight check is more reliable than breast fullness or feed length alone.

The HHS guide to making breast milk explains how diaper output, alert periods, contentment after feeds, and weight checks fit together.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician or a lactation specialist

  • The same latch problem keeps returning, baby strongly favors one side, or you want someone to watch a full feeding.
  • You are unsure whether baby is swallowing or getting enough. A feeding observation and weight check can answer more than timing a feed.

Get prompt medical advice

  • Pain lasts through feeds, nipples crack or bleed, baby repeatedly slips off or clicks, or sucking stays light and fluttery.
  • Once milk volume has increased, there is little or no swallowing; baby remains hungry after most feeds, frequently misses feeds, or feeds fewer than 8 times in 24 hours.
  • Wet diapers are not increasing, urine is dark, there has been no urine for more than 8 hours, or skin looks increasingly deep yellow or orange.
  • Milk has not noticeably increased by about day 5, or severe breast fullness keeps baby from attaching.

The AAP’s warning signs of breastfeeding problems support getting help promptly rather than waiting for pain or poor transfer to resolve on its own.

Get urgent help now

Call emergency services if baby cannot be woken, is not moving, or is very weak. If baby is hard to wake or too weak to suck, seek emergency care now. If baby will not breastfeed or takes very little for more than 8 hours, seek medical care now.

These thresholds follow the AAP breastfeeding “when to call” guidance.

Usually not a worry

  • Cluster feeding: several feeds close together, even hourly for a stretch, can be normal when swallowing, diapers, and weight are reassuring.
  • Uneven feed lengths: feeds do not need to last the same number of minutes. Watch active swallowing and baby’s cues instead of the clock alone.
  • Quiet early feeds: small colostrum swallows can be difficult to hear. This changes the advice if baby is also too sleepy to feed, misses feeds, or has poor diaper output.
  • Different breasts, different patterns: one side may flow faster or make more milk, and baby may have a preference. Repeated refusal, pain, or poor intake deserves help.

Useful today

  1. Offer the breast at early cues such as stirring, rooting, or hands to mouth; a calm baby may latch more easily than a crying baby.
  2. For one feed, check only four things: body aligned, mouth wide, chin touching, and rhythmic swallows.
  3. If it pinches, slide a clean finger into the corner of baby’s mouth to break suction, then try again. Do not pull baby straight off the nipple.
  4. Use skin-to-skin and try another comfortable hold if either of you becomes frustrated.
  5. Keep a simple note of feeds and diapers, and arrange hands-on help today if pain or milk transfer remains uncertain.
