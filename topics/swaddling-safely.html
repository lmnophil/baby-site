Swaddling safely

Swaddling is optional: it may calm a young baby, but it does not lower the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). If you use a swaddle, start every sleep on the back in a firm, flat, bare infant sleep space—and stop at the first sign of trying to roll.

A safe swaddle right now

In the early newborn weeks, some babies settle in a light wrap and others prefer to move their arms. Either is fine. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) swaddling guide recommends these checks whenever a baby is swaddled.

Back, bare, and loose at the hips

  • Back: place baby face-up for every nap and night sleep—never on the side or stomach while swaddled.
  • Bare: use a separate crib, bassinet, portable crib, or play yard with a firm, flat, level mattress and only a fitted sheet. Add no loose blanket, pillow, positioner, toy, or extra covering.
  • Secure: keep the wrap away from baby’s face and make sure it cannot loosen. Never use a weighted swaddle, weighted blanket, or another weighted object.
  • Room to move: leave space for the hips and knees to bend up and out. Straightening and tightly wrapping the legs can harm developing hips.
  • Use a thin blanket or a correctly sized wearable swaddle, following its instructions and limits.
  • Fit the chest snugly, not tightly: two or three fingers should slide between baby’s chest and the wrap. Breathing should look easy.
  • Count the swaddle as a clothing layer. The AAP safe-sleep recommendations suggest no more than one extra layer compared with what an adult would wear in the same room. Skip an indoor hat.
  • Check baby’s chest and the wrap after settling them down. Remove a layer if baby is sweaty, has a hot chest, or looks flushed.

What changes next

  1. During the early weeks: keep reassessing the fit and whether baby is trying to turn. Swaddling may soothe, but longer sleep is not proof that it is safer.
  2. At the first attempt to roll: stop every blanket swaddle and every wearable product that compresses the arms, chest, or body. Do not wait for a complete roll. A deliberate turn toward the side or repeated attempts to get a shoulder or hip over are enough to make the change.
  3. The timing varies: rolling often appears around 3 to 4 months, but attempts can begin as early as 2 months. Use what baby does—not the calendar—as the stop signal.
  4. After swaddling: switch to fitted sleep clothing or a non-weighted sleep sack that leaves the arms and body free to move. Continue placing baby on the back at the start of every sleep.

NIH Safe to Sleep also advises stopping once rolling begins because a swaddled baby may not be able to move out of a side or stomach position. Its swaddling and weighted-product answers explain why back sleeping and an unweighted setup matter.

What a reassuring fit looks like

Swaddling is going as intended when several observable checks stay true:

  • Baby begins each sleep on their back and is not trying to roll.
  • The fabric stays secure and clear of the mouth, nose, and head.
  • Two or three fingers fit at the chest, breathing looks comfortable, and baby’s hips and knees can flex freely.
  • Baby’s chest feels comfortably warm rather than hot, and the skin is dry rather than sweaty.
  • The sleep space remains empty except for its fitted sheet.

Sleep length is not a safety check. A baby may still wake often, startle, or prefer not to be wrapped; keep the safe setup the same either way.

When to ask or act

Call the pediatrician

Ask during office hours if the wrap repeatedly loosens or seems too tight, if baby’s hips do not move freely, or if a medical condition or hospital plan makes the usual sleep guidance difficult to follow. A nurse or pediatrician can watch your technique and suggest an uncompressed alternative.

Stop and get prompt medical advice

Unwrap baby and seek prompt advice if they remain unusually hard to wake, keep breathing rapidly, or stay sweaty, flushed, or hot after extra layers are removed. Stop swaddling for all future sleep as soon as any attempt to roll appears.

Get urgent help now

Remove anything covering the face. If baby is blue or gray, is struggling to breathe, is unresponsive, is not breathing, or is only gasping, call 911 now. Begin infant CPR according to your training or the dispatcher’s instructions; the American Red Cross child and baby first-aid guide outlines the emergency response.

Usually not a worry

  • Arms in or out: before any rolling attempt, the AAP reports no evidence that either arm position changes SIDS risk. The back position, secure fit, free hips, and bare sleep space still matter.
  • Not liking the swaddle: some babies settle better without one. Fitted sleep clothing is enough; swaddling is not a required milestone.
  • An escaped hand: this does not mean the chest or legs should be wrapped tighter. Refit with safe spacing, use the correct product size, or stop swaddling. Stop immediately if fabric can reach the face.
  • A no-swaddling child-care rule: some care settings do not allow it because several caregivers may use different techniques. It is reasonable for home and child-care routines to differ while both follow safe-sleep rules.

Useful today

  1. Choose one thin, non-weighted wrap in the correct size; read its instructions and limits.
  2. Do the finger and hip-movement checks before the next sleep, then confirm the face stays clear after baby is put down.
  3. Remove extra blankets, positioners, and soft items from the sleep space.
  4. Put an arms-free sleep sack or fitted sleep clothing aside now so the rolling transition does not require improvising later.
  5. Give every caregiver the same rule: back for every sleep, and no swaddling once baby tries to roll.
