Feeding

Bottle feeding

Hold baby for every bottle, let sucking set the pace, and stop when baby shows they are full—even when milk remains. Newborn bottle-feeding is a skill that becomes steadier with practice; comfort, breathing, diaper output, and growth matter more than finishing a fixed amount.

What a bottle can look like right now

In the first day or two, a bottle-fed newborn may take only about ½ ounce (15 mL) at a feed; after that, many take 1–2 ounces (30–60 mL). These are broad guides, not a target to force. Babies born early or with jaundice, low blood sugar, poor weight gain, or another medical concern may need an individual feeding plan. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) explains early feeding amounts and patterns.

  1. Start calmly: offer the bottle at early hunger cues such as hands to mouth, turning toward the bottle, lip smacking, or clenched hands. Crying is a late cue.
  2. Hold baby close: support the head and neck, keep the face visible, and hold the bottle at an angle rather than straight up so milk flows when baby sucks.
  3. Build in pauses: lower the bottle when baby stops sucking, turns away, or needs a breathing break. Resume if hunger cues return.
  4. Let baby finish: a closed mouth, head turned away, relaxed hands, or repeated stopping can mean “all done.” Do not coax baby to empty the bottle.

This cue-led approach follows the CDC’s bottle-feeding guidance and its newborn hunger and fullness cues.

The short safety check

  • Put only breast milk or infant formula in a newborn’s bottle—no cereal, food, water, or other drinks unless a clinician directs otherwise.
  • Never prop a bottle, leave it in baby’s mouth, or put baby to bed with it. Stay close and hold the bottle throughout the feed.
  • Take every bottle apart and clean all parts after each feed. Air-dry them completely.
  • Clean bottles after every feed. Sanitize them at least daily if baby is younger than 2 months, was born prematurely, or has a weakened immune system; a dishwasher with hot water and heated drying or a sanitizing setting can do both jobs.

See the CDC’s bottle cleaning and sanitizing instructions for dishwasher, hand-washing, boiling, steaming, and storage steps.

What changes next

  • Over the next several days: sucking, swallowing, and breathing may become more coordinated. A feed that once needed many pauses may settle into short runs of steady drinking followed by clear breaks.
  • Across the first month: many babies gradually take more at once and leave longer gaps. By the end of the month, many take about 3–4 ounces every 3–4 hours. Hunger cues and the baby’s care plan still come before the clock.
  • Whenever the pattern shifts: offer a modest amount first, pause at fullness cues, and offer more if clear hunger cues continue. Do not increase every bottle simply because one feed was bigger.

These ranges come from the AAP’s feeding guide; they describe common transitions, not deadlines.

Reassuring signs

Look for a cluster of signs across the day rather than judging one bottle:

  • Baby wakes or can be gently roused, starts with active sucking, and pauses to breathe without distress.
  • Drinking looks rhythmic rather than frantic; baby’s color and breathing remain normal and milk is not pouring from the mouth.
  • Baby gives recognizable hunger and fullness cues and seems relaxed after at least some feeds.
  • Wet diapers increase over the first days. After day 4–5, at least 5–6 wet diapers a day is a useful intake clue; the whole feeding and growth picture matters too.
  • Weight begins to trend upward at follow-up checks. The growth trend is a better intake check than one bottle’s volume.

The diaper and growth clues are from the AAP’s feeding guide; use the clinician’s plan for a baby with feeding or growth concerns.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • You want help choosing a bottle or nipple, understanding the feeding plan, or checking whether a new pattern is expected.
  • Feeds are usually stressful, very dribbly, or hard to coordinate even though baby otherwise seems well.

Get prompt medical advice

  • Baby repeatedly coughs, chokes, gulps frantically, develops noisy or harder breathing, or cannot stay coordinated during feeds.
  • Baby suddenly takes much less, repeatedly refuses feeds, has a weak suck, becomes harder to wake, or vomits repeatedly rather than having ordinary small spit-ups.
  • Wet diapers stop increasing in the first days, fall below the expected pattern, or weight is not trending as the care team expected.

Get urgent help now

Call emergency services if baby’s lips, tongue, or face look blue or gray; baby is limp or unresponsive; breathing stops; or the chest pulls in with each breath. Call the pediatrician immediately for a baby 3 months or younger with a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, even without other symptoms.

The AAP’s urgent and emergency-signs guide and its infant fever guidance support these directions.

Usually not a worry

  • Different amounts at different feeds: appetite varies. A smaller bottle can be fine when baby wakes for feeds, drinks actively, has reassuring diapers, and grows as expected.
  • Milk left in the bottle: stopping at fullness cues is the goal, not waste or a failed feed. The CDC specifically advises against forcing the last milk.
  • Wanting to suck after a feed: sucking can be comforting as well as a hunger sign. Pause, hold baby, and look for several hunger cues before offering more.
  • An occasional sputter while learning: stop the flow, sit baby comfortably, and let breathing settle. Repeated coughing, color change, breathing effort, or distress changes the advice—get medical guidance.

Useful today

  1. During one feed, watch only three things: steady breathing, natural pauses, and the first fullness cue.
  2. Offer a smaller starting amount and add more only if hunger cues continue after a pause.
  3. Keep a brief note of feed times, approximate amounts, and wet diapers to share at the next newborn check.
  4. Take the bottle apart after the feed; wash and air-dry it. Add daily sanitizing if baby is under 2 months, was born prematurely, or has a weakened immune system.
  5. Show every caregiver the same rule: baby is held, the bottle is held, and baby decides when the feed ends.
