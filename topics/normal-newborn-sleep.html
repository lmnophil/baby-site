Sleep

What is normal newborn sleep?

Most newborns sleep about 16–17 hours across a full day, usually in short stretches around the clock rather than one long night. There is wide variation, but every sleep should start on baby's back in a separate, firm, flat, level sleep space with only a fitted sheet.

Right now: lots of sleep, little pattern

In the first days and weeks, sleep and waking are spread through both day and night. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says most newborns sleep 16–17 hours in 24 hours and may wake at the end of a sleep cycle as often as every one or two hours. Some babies sleep more and others less, so the total alone does not show whether a baby is well. Feeding, alertness, color, breathing, diapers, and growth matter too. AAP guidance on newborn sleep cycles and NHS guidance on newborn sleep explain these patterns.

Newborn sleep has two easy-to-notice looks:

  • Active or light sleep: eyelids may flutter, the face may move, and baby may suck, startle, twitch, whimper, groan, or briefly cry. Breathing can sound uneven.
  • Quiet or deep sleep: baby is more still, breathing is steadier, and waking takes more effort.

A baby can move between these states within one sleep. The AAP's guide to normal newborn sleep movements and sounds can help distinguish active sleep from a concerning change.

Every sleep, every caregiver

Place baby on their back in a safety-approved crib, bassinet, or portable play yard near the adult bed. The surface should be firm, flat, level, and covered only by a fitted sheet—no pillows, blankets, positioners, bumpers, or toys. Adult beds, couches, and armchairs are not safe infant sleep spaces. These NIH Safe to Sleep recommendations apply to naps as well as nighttime.

What changes next

  • Over the first few weeks: expect many short sleeps, frequent waking to feed, and no dependable bedtime. A transition is showing a few more calm, alert periods—not sleeping through the night.
  • Over the next several weeks: some sleep stretches may lengthen and begin to fall more often at night. You may notice that nighttime feeds become quieter and baby returns to sleep more readily.
  • Around 3–6 months: some babies need fewer night feeds and sleep for longer blocks, while others continue to wake often. The signal is a gradually longer nighttime stretch alongside good feeding and growth, not reaching a date on the calendar.

These are broad guides, not deadlines. Sleep may become irregular again during growth spurts or illness, and the NHS emphasizes that babies develop their own patterns.

Reassuring signs

Look at the whole day rather than one nap:

  • Baby wakes independently or can be gently roused for feeds according to the care team's plan.
  • Baby sucks and swallows during feeds and has the expected diaper and weight trend.
  • There are at least some calm, alert moments, even if they are brief.
  • Baby moves normally when awake, has their usual color, and breathes comfortably.
  • The amount of sleep may vary, but there is no abrupt change in feeding, alertness, or behavior.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • You are unsure how long baby may sleep between feeds or when waking is still needed.
  • Baby's sleep pattern is making feeding difficult, even though baby otherwise seems well.
  • Exhaustion is making it hard for you to keep feeds and sleep safe; ask the care team about practical support.

Get prompt medical advice

  • A newborn is suddenly sleeping more than usual, needs much more waking for feeds, cannot finish feeds, or has a clear change in feeding.
  • Baby looks or acts abnormal in any way, especially during the first month.
  • Breathing becomes hard, a breathing pause lasts longer than 10 seconds, or repeated jerking happens while baby is awake or lasts longer than 10 seconds.

Get urgent help now

Call emergency services if baby cannot be woken, is not moving or is very weak, has blue or gray lips, tongue, or face, struggles for each breath, or makes a new moaning or grunting sound with every breath. Seek emergency care now if baby is hard to wake, suddenly floppy, or has a weak suck or cannot suck for long.

These warning signs follow the AAP's newborn illness guidance.

Usually not a worry

  • No day-and-night schedule: newborns do not yet organize sleep around the household clock. Frequent night waking is expected.
  • Noisy, wiggly sleep: brief whimpers, groans, sucking motions, startles, or twitches can be active sleep when baby remains comfortable and their color stays normal.
  • Brief uneven breathing: pauses shorter than 10 seconds followed by a few faster breaths can be normal if baby stays comfortable and does not change color. Longer pauses, hard breathing, or color change need medical help.
  • Waking when put down: many newborns fall asleep while held and stir when moved. It is fine to soothe them again, but put them in their own safe sleep space before the caregiver sleeps.
  • An uneven total: one day may include more or less sleep than the next. A sudden change paired with poor feeding or unusual behavior matters more than comparison with another baby.

Useful today

  1. Do a quick sleep-space check: back position, firm flat level surface, fitted sheet only, and baby's face and head uncovered.
  2. Follow baby's feeding and waking plan. If the plan is unclear, use the feeding frequency and waking guide to prepare questions for the pediatrician.
  3. When baby stirs or makes a brief sound with eyes closed, pause long enough to see whether they settle—while checking that color and breathing remain normal.
  4. Use daylight and ordinary household sound during the day; keep nighttime feeds dim, quiet, and calm. Do not expect a schedule yet.
  5. Plan for adult fatigue. Avoid feeding on a couch or armchair if you might doze. If sleep happens during a feed, return baby to their own sleep space as soon as you wake.
