Diaper rash

Most diaper rash is irritated skin from moisture, stool, or rubbing. It usually looks like pink or red, dry or tender patches where the diaper touches, and gentle care should start making it better within two to three days.

For a young newborn: contact the pediatrician about a new diaper-area rash during the first six weeks. Get prompt medical advice for blisters, pimples, yellow crusts, spreading redness, or a baby who seems unwell. MedlinePlus gives extra-cautious guidance for rashes in the first six weeks.

What it may look like right now

The common irritant type forms patches on the buttocks, genitals, lower belly, or upper thighs—skin that has been against the diaper. The groin creases often stay clearer because they have less contact with urine and stool. Mild skin may be dry or slightly tender; a more severe rash can look raw or bleed. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) compares common diaper-rash patterns.

Check for a change from the baby's usual skin color as well as texture, warmth, raw areas, and discomfort during cleaning. Color alone can be hard to judge under cream or on different skin tones.

What should change next

  1. Over the next several changes: less time in a wet or soiled diaper, gentle cleaning, air-drying, and a barrier paste should stop adding irritation.
  2. Within two to three days: an ordinary irritant rash should look less red or raw, cover less skin, and bother the baby less. No clear improvement—or any worsening—is a reason to call.
  3. If the pattern changes: shiny, bright pink or red skin with sharp edges, small bumps beyond the main patch, and redness deep in the groin folds can suggest Candida (yeast), especially after antibiotics. Yellow crusts, weeping, pus, sores, or spreading painful redness can signal a bacterial infection. These clues need a clinician's assessment rather than more trial-and-error creams.

Reassuring signs

  • The rash is mild, limited to the diaper-contact area, and the folds look clear.
  • There are no blisters, open sores, pus, yellow crusts, or quickly spreading areas.
  • The baby feeds and wakes as usual and settles when the area is not being cleaned.
  • Each day, the patch is smaller, less raw, or less uncomfortable.

Look at the trend and the whole baby rather than expecting the skin to clear after one change.

When to get medical help

Call the pediatrician

  • The rash is not clearly improving after two to three days of gentle care.
  • It spreads beyond the diaper area, becomes very raw, or bleeds.
  • It involves the folds with sharp-edged redness or small surrounding bumps.
  • It keeps returning, started after antibiotics, or follows every use of a product.

Get prompt medical advice

  • There are blisters, pimples, boils, open sores, yellow crusts, oozing, or pus.
  • Redness is spreading or the rash is very painful.
  • The baby is hard to console, feeding differently, unusually sleepy, or seems ill.

For a baby under one month, grouped tiny fluid-filled blisters or pimples, yellow scabs, spreading redness, or abnormal behavior need care now. See the AAP diaper-rash symptom guidance.

Get urgent help now

If a baby under three months has a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, call the pediatrician immediately—even if the baby otherwise seems well. Also get urgent help for widespread blistering or peeling skin, trouble breathing, blue or gray color, inability to wake, or a baby who looks very ill. The AAP explains fever thresholds for young infants. The AAP also explains when a rash or other symptom needs emergency care.

Usually not worth stressing about

Diaper rash is extremely common and can happen despite attentive care. A small, diaper-shaped patch that stays out of the folds and begins easing with gentle care is usually reasonable to watch at home. Barrier paste also does not need to be scrubbed completely away at every change: if it is clean, add another layer. The advice changes if the rash spreads, opens, blisters, crusts, drains, causes marked pain, or comes with fever or a baby who seems unwell.

Useful today

  1. Change wet or soiled diapers promptly, especially after stool.
  2. Rinse gently with warm water and a soft cloth; fragrance-free, alcohol-free wipes are another option for mild skin. Do not rub or scrub. Pat or air-dry.
  3. Apply a thick, fragrance-free zinc oxide or petrolatum barrier. Add more over clean paste instead of rubbing it all off.
  4. Fasten a clean, absorbent diaper loosely. A short, supervised diaper-free break on a washable towel can help the skin dry.
  5. Skip talcum powder, fragranced products, and antiseptics. Ask the pediatrician before using steroid, antifungal, or antibiotic medicine on a young baby's rash.

These steps follow the AAP's diaper-rash care advice and MedlinePlus guidance on gentle cleaning and barrier products.
