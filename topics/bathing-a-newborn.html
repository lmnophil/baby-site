Bathing a newborn

Until the umbilical cord stump is gone and the navel is healed, clean baby with a sponge bath rather than putting them in water. A few baths a week is usually plenty—and whenever baby is near water, keep a hand on them and never step away.

A sponge bath right now

There is no need to rush the first bath or make bathing a daily job. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guide to bathing a baby relays the recommendation to delay the first bath for at least 24 hours when possible. Once home, thorough diaper-area cleaning at each change plus about three baths a week is generally enough; more frequent bathing can dry newborn skin.

  1. Set up first: choose a warm, draft-free space. Put lukewarm water, washcloths, a towel, a clean diaper, and clothes within reach. A mild, fragrance-free baby cleanser is optional.
  2. Keep baby secure and warm: lay baby on a comfortable flat surface. If the surface is raised, use its safety strap and keep one hand on baby. Wrap baby in a dry towel and uncover only the area being washed.
  3. Work from clean to dirty: wipe the face with plain water, then clean behind the ears, under the chin, in neck and body creases, and around fingers and toes. Use cleanser only where needed, rinse it away, and wash the diaper area last.
  4. Protect the cord: keep the stump above the water and dry. If it gets soiled, clean it gently with soap and water, then dry it carefully; do not pull it off.
  5. Finish warmly: pat the folds dry, then diaper and dress baby. If skin seems dry, use a small amount of fragrance-free moisturizer.

The non-negotiable water check

  • Test water with the inside of your wrist or elbow. It should feel warm, not hot. Set the water heater so the hottest water at the faucet is no more than 120°F (49°C).
  • Fill the bowl or tub first and turn off the faucet before baby goes near the water; running water can change temperature suddenly.
  • Keep one hand on baby whenever water is present. If you need to reach for something, answer the door, or leave for any reason, take baby with you.
  • Do not count on a bath seat, sling, or tub to prevent drowning. Babies can drown silently in 1–2 inches of water within seconds.

See the AAP’s current infant drowning prevention and bath safety guidance.

What changes next

  • Over the next days: the cord stump usually dries, darkens, shrivels, and separates on its own. Many fall off in about one or two weeks; the AAP’s cord guidance describes one to three weeks as a normal range.
  • When the stump is gone: wait until the navel looks dry and healed, without a moist or raw area. Continuing sponge baths for a few more days is fine.
  • For the first tub bath: use a stable infant tub with about 2 inches of warm water. Support the head and neck, lower baby feet first, keep the face above water, and keep one hand on baby. Reassess the setup as baby becomes wigglier; hands-on supervision never changes.

These are guides, not deadlines. A dry, healed cord area—not a particular day—is the signal for moving to the tub. The AAP’s umbilical cord guidance explains the usual changes and when the navel needs attention.

Reassuring signs

Look at the whole pattern rather than judging one bath:

  • Baby’s face stays above the water, breathing and color look usual, and baby warms up after being dried and dressed.
  • Skin folds and the diaper area are clean and dry without vigorous rubbing.
  • The cord is becoming drier and darker, while the skin around its base keeps its usual color.
  • Baby may protest being undressed or washed but settles when wrapped, held, fed, or soothed.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • You are unsure when tub baths are safe after a procedure or special cord-care instruction.
  • Skin stays very dry or irritated even after less-frequent bathing and fragrance-free skin care.
  • The cord remains attached beyond the usual one-to-three-week range, or the navel is not becoming dry after it separates.

Get prompt medical advice

  • The navel has lots of drainage, cloudy discharge or pus, or nearby pimples, blisters, or sores.
  • A small amount of bleeding keeps returning for more than three days.
  • Hot water or a product caused painful redness, swelling, raw skin, or a blister.

Get urgent help now

Seek urgent medical care if redness spreads from the navel, a red streak appears, or the area becomes swollen, tender, or foul-smelling. Also seek care now if cord bleeding does not stop after two 10-minute periods of direct pressure. A rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby younger than 3 months needs medical care now; do not give fever medicine before baby is evaluated. The AAP’s infant fever guidance gives the threshold and response.

If baby is removed from water and is unresponsive or not breathing normally, call emergency services. Start infant CPR with rescue breaths and chest compressions if trained, or follow the dispatcher’s instructions immediately. The AAP and American Heart Association drowning response guidance says anyone needing resuscitation after submersion should be evaluated at a hospital.

Usually not a worry

  • Bathing only a few times a week: newborns do not need a daily bath when the face, neck folds, and diaper area are cleaned as needed.
  • Crying during a bath: keep it brief, wrap and comfort baby, and try another day. Returning to sponge baths for a while is fine.
  • Washing over the soft spots: gentle cleaning of the scalp with a soft washcloth is safe. Do not scrub or pick at flakes.
  • A little dry skin: use less cleanser, bathe less often, and try a fragrance-free moisturizer. Raw, oozing, or blistered skin needs a call.

Useful today

  1. Choose sponge bath or spot-cleaning if the cord is still attached or the navel is not fully healed.
  2. Place every supply within reach before undressing baby.
  3. Check the water with your wrist or elbow and confirm the faucet is off.
  4. Clean one fold at a time, keeping the rest of baby wrapped.
  5. Show every caregiver the same rule: one hand stays on baby whenever water is present.
