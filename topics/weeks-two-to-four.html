What’s next

What changes in weeks 2–4?

Weeks 2–4 are still very much newborn life, but growth becomes easier to see and quiet, alert moments may gradually last longer. The pace varies: these changes are broad guides, not a schedule every baby must follow.

Right now: the pattern matters most

Milk feeds, scattered sleep, crying, and diaper changes still shape most of the day. A predictable routine is not expected. Look instead for a baby who wakes or can be roused to feed, has an established diaper pattern, and returns to calm, alert, or sleeping periods between needs.

Weight is an important trend in this window. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) first-month growth guide says babies should generally return to birth weight by about 2 weeks, and almost all do by 3 weeks. A clinician’s repeat weights are more useful than judging one feed, one diaper, or how baby looks.

Keep these safety rules steady

  • A rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby 3 months or younger needs an immediate call to the pediatrician and urgent evaluation, even if baby otherwise seems well. A rectal reading is the most reliable at this age; see the AAP’s infant fever guidance.
  • For every sleep, place baby on their back in their own crib, bassinet, or play yard on a firm, flat, level surface with only a fitted sheet. Keep blankets, pillows, toys, positioners, and other people out of the sleep space. Follow the NIH Safe to Sleep recommendations.

What may change next

  • Around week 2: weight should be moving back toward birth weight. The cord stump may dry and fall off; the usual range is 1–3 weeks. Keep the area clean and dry, and let it separate on its own. The AAP umbilical cord care guide says a stump still attached after 3 weeks needs a doctor’s visit.
  • During weeks 2–3: baby may spend more time quietly looking at a nearby face or listening to a familiar voice. Head control is still limited, and movements can remain jerky or reflexive.
  • During weeks 3–4: faster growth commonly occurs sometime between 3 and 6 weeks. You may also notice more stretching, hands coming toward the mouth, and a little more response to your voice.
  • Across this whole window: crying may increase. AAP guidance notes that heavier crying often starts around 2 weeks; it does not mean a caregiver is doing something wrong. Feeding, holding, gentle rocking, or singing may help, and sometimes baby will still cry. See the AAP’s support for new parents.

By the end of the first month, many babies are more alert and responsive, watch a person holding them, react to sounds, and focus best on things about 8–12 inches away. The AAP’s 1-month milestone guide describes these as typical signs, not deadlines.

Reassuring progress

Look for a cluster of signs rather than one perfect day:

  • Baby wakes or can be gently roused for feeds and returns to their usual state afterward.
  • Feeding and wet-diaper patterns are steady for this baby, without a sudden drop.
  • Repeat weights show growth moving in the expected direction.
  • There are gradually more quiet-alert moments or responses to close faces, voices, and sounds.
  • Breathing looks comfortable, and the lips and tongue keep their usual color.
  • The cord area becomes dry and clean after the stump falls away.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • Baby is not back at birth weight by about 2 weeks, or is nearing 3 weeks without doing so.
  • The cord is still attached, or the navel is not healed and dry, after 3 weeks.
  • You have concerns about feeding, hearing, movement, alertness, or what to expect at the next checkup.

Get prompt medical advice

  • Baby suddenly feeds less, cannot finish feeds, has a weaker suck, or is sleeping much more than usual.
  • Baby has new coughing, repeated vomiting or diarrhea, a weak or unusual cry, or simply looks or acts newly unwell.
  • The rectal temperature is 100.4°F (38°C) or higher: call immediately and arrange urgent in-person care.

Illness can be subtle in the first month. The AAP newborn illness guide advises calling promptly for a new change in feeding, appearance, or behavior.

Get urgent help now

Call 911 or your local emergency number if baby cannot be woken, is not moving or is very weak, struggles for each breath, makes a new grunt or moan with each breath, or has blue or gray lips, tongue, or face.

Go to an emergency department now for green vomit, new floppiness or decreased movement, a weak suck that does not last, or if baby is very hard to wake. This page does not replace a clinician or local emergency services.

Usually not a worry

  • Jerky motions, clenched fists, or a wobbly head: these remain common at one month; always support the head and neck.
  • Eyes that briefly wander or cross: this can still happen at this age. Mention an eye that stays turned in or out to the pediatrician.
  • Small facial pimples: “baby acne” often appears around weeks 3–5 and usually clears with time and gentle washing.
  • An uneven pace: alertness, crying, and new responses can change from day to day. One quieter day does not erase progress if feeding, movement, color, and waking remain usual.

These variations need a call when they come with fever, poor feeding, unusual sleepiness, difficult breathing, or a clear change from baby’s usual behavior.

Useful today

  1. Notice the overall feeding, diaper, waking, and alertness pattern; write it down only if a trend is changing or you want to ask about it.
  2. During a calm, awake moment, hold your face nearby, talk softly, and give baby time to look or respond.
  3. Check that the cord or healing navel is dry and that the next pediatric visit is on the calendar.
  4. Reset the sleep space before every sleep: back position, firm and flat surface, fitted sheet, nothing else.
  5. Make a crying handoff plan. If you feel overwhelmed, place baby safely on their back in the empty sleep space, step away briefly, and ask another trusted adult for help.
