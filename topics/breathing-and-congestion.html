Health & safety

Breathing and congestion

Newborn breathing can be irregular, snuffly, and surprisingly noisy without being dangerous. Look beyond the sound: if baby is struggling for each breath, can barely cry, stops breathing, or has blue or gray lips, tongue, or face, call 911 or your local emergency number now.

What breathing can look like right now

Watch baby while calm, not crying or feeding. The belly may move more than the chest, and the rhythm may speed up and slow down. A comfortable pause shorter than 10 seconds followed by a few quicker breaths is called periodic breathing. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says a resting newborn rate should be under 60 breaths per minute; brief faster bursts can be normal when they settle within about a minute. See the AAP’s guide to normal newborn breathing patterns and sounds.

Snorts, whistles, and sneezes often come from dried mucus in tiny nasal passages, not a cold. Nasal blockage matters when it makes sucking and breathing during a feed difficult. Wheezing from the chest, a harsh sound while breathing in, grunting with every breath, or skin pulling in around the ribs is not ordinary congestion.

Effort matters more than volume

Comfortable breathing leaves the skin between and below the ribs relaxed. Nostrils should not flare with each breath, the head should not bob, and lips and tongue should keep their usual color. Skin pulling inward between the ribs or at the base of the neck is called a retraction and means baby is working to breathe.

What may change next

  • After a sneeze or saline: dried mucus may loosen, the nasal sound may fade, and feeding may become easier. The sound can return without meaning baby is ill.
  • Over the first month: brief periodic breathing usually becomes less noticeable and often resolves around 1 month. This is a guide, not a deadline; comfort, color, and feeding remain the useful checks.
  • If an infection is developing: congestion may be followed over the next few days by cough, poorer feeding, or harder breathing. With RSV, symptoms are often worst on days 3–5 and average 7–14 days, according to the AAP’s RSV guide for babies. A newborn should not wait out that timeline: call promptly about new illness signs.

Reassuring signs

  • Breathing looks easy when baby is calm, without retractions, nostril flaring, head bobbing, or repeated grunting.
  • Lips and tongue keep their usual color.
  • Baby wakes or can be gently roused, latches or takes the bottle, and can finish feeds close to the usual pattern.
  • Wet diapers continue near the expected pattern, and baby has some alert periods.
  • Nasal noise comes and goes or improves after a sneeze, plain saline, or gentle suction.

A reassuring sign does not cancel a fever, a long breathing pause, or visible work to breathe.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • Mild stuffiness keeps returning, but breathing and feeding remain comfortable.
  • Plain saline does not clear the nose, or you want the care team to check a recurring sound.
  • You are unsure how to count breaths or use the suction bulb supplied by the hospital.

Get prompt medical advice

Call the pediatrician now or use the after-hours line if:

  • a baby under 1 month has a new cough or congestion and looks or acts different, feeds poorly, is harder to wake, or has poor color;
  • breathing stays at 60 or more breaths per minute while calm, or a pause lasts longer than 10 seconds even if breathing has restarted;
  • congestion prevents finishing feeds, activity drops noticeably, or there is no wet diaper for 8 hours; or
  • a baby 3 months or younger has a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher. Call immediately, even if baby seems well, as the AAP explains in its fever guidance for babies.

If baby is getting worse or you cannot reach a clinician promptly, seek in-person urgent care.

Get urgent help now

Call 911 or your local emergency number if baby:

  • has stopped breathing, cannot be woken, or is very weak;
  • is struggling for each breath, can barely make a sound or cry, or makes a new grunt with every breath; or
  • has blue or gray lips, tongue, or face.

Go to an emergency department now for trouble breathing in a baby under 1 year, ribs pulling in with each breath, or loud wheezing or a harsh sound while breathing in. The AAP’s breathing-trouble guide gives these emergency thresholds. Call 911 if the trouble is severe or worsening.

Usually not a worry

  • A brief pause: less than 10 seconds can be periodic breathing when baby stays comfortable and keeps the usual color. A longer pause changes the advice.
  • Occasional sneezing or snuffling: this often clears dust or dried mucus. Call if breathing becomes hard or feeding is affected.
  • Gurgles during sleep: air moving through saliva or a little refluxed milk can sound dramatic. Repeated grunting with each breath is different and urgent.
  • A short burst of quicker breaths: it can happen in a healthy newborn if it settles within about a minute. Breathing that stays fast while calm needs a call.

Useful today

  1. If breathing seems fast, wait until baby is calm and count each belly or chest rise for one full minute.
  2. For a blocked nose, use one drop of plain, unmedicated saline in one nostril. Squeeze the bulb before bringing it to the nostril, suction gently, then repeat on the other side. Use this only when needed—especially before a feed—and no more than four times a day. The AAP’s breathing-trouble guide gives this newborn-specific routine.
  3. Keep sleep flat and firm with baby on their back. Do not incline the mattress or add pillows, towels, or positioners for congestion. The AAP’s safe sleep guidance for a congested baby explains why an incline can narrow the airway.
  4. Avoid smoke, dust, strong scents, medicated nose drops, and cotton swabs inside the nostrils.
  5. Save the pediatrician’s daytime and after-hours numbers. A short video of a recurring sound can help the clinician, but do not delay urgent care to record one.
