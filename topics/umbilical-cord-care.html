Umbilical cord care

In the first days, the cord stump needs simple care: keep it clean, dry, and outside the diaper, then let it fall off on its own. Infection is uncommon, but spreading redness, foul drainage, fever, or an unwell newborn needs quick medical attention.

What it may look like right now

A fresh stump may look shiny and yellowish. As it dries, it becomes smaller, firmer, and brown, gray, or nearly black. That color change is part of normal drying—not a reason to clean it off.

Use clean, dry care

  • Wash your hands before and after touching the area.
  • Fold the diaper below the stump so air can reach it and urine cannot soak it.
  • If pee or poop gets on it, wash gently with soap and water, then pat it dry.
  • Use sponge baths until the stump is off and the belly button is dry.
  • Do not pull, pick, bandage, or cover the stump.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says routine rubbing alcohol is not needed, and the World Health Organization recommends clean, dry cord care. Do not add peroxide, ointment, powder, oil, or another home product unless baby’s clinician gave a specific plan. See the AAP’s umbilical cord care guidance and the WHO postnatal care guideline.

What changes next

  • Over the next several days: the stump should become darker, drier, and more shriveled.
  • Usually within 1–3 weeks: it loosens and drops off. Let it separate naturally, even if it is hanging by a thread.
  • After it falls: the center may look a little raw or red, form a small scab, or leave a few drops of blood. It should then become drier over the next several days; keep the diaper edge below it while it heals.

Published cutoffs differ slightly. The AAP advises a doctor’s visit if the stump is still attached at 3 weeks; MedlinePlus advises contacting the clinician at 4 weeks. Calling around 3 weeks is reasonable and does not mean there is an emergency if baby otherwise seems well.

Reassuring signs

  • The stump is getting drier and smaller rather than wetter or swollen.
  • The surrounding belly skin keeps its usual color, with no redness spreading outward.
  • There is no foul smell, pus-like drainage, or tenderness around the base.
  • After separation, a tiny blood spot or scab dries instead of repeatedly refilling with blood.
  • Baby feeds and wakes in their usual newborn pattern and does not seem ill.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • The stump is still attached at about 3 weeks.
  • After it falls off, a small moist pink or red bump or light-yellow fluid lasts about a week. This may be an umbilical granuloma, which a clinician can check.
  • You are unsure whether a hospital or discharge instruction changes routine dry care.

Get prompt medical advice

  • Redness spreads from the belly button, or the nearby skin is swollen or tender.
  • There is foul-smelling yellow drainage, pus, or pain that makes baby cry when the area is touched.
  • Bleeding is active—another drop appears each time one is wiped away.
  • There is a large amount of fluid, or drainage looks like urine.

These can be signs of infection or another cord problem. Cord infection can spread quickly, so do not wait for a routine visit. The AAP lists redness, foul yellow drainage, and tenderness as infection signs; its umbilical cord symptom checker says spreading redness needs care now and lots of drainage needs medical advice within 24 hours.

Get urgent help now

Seek urgent medical care now if a cord concern comes with a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, poor feeding, unusual difficulty waking, weakness, or a floppy body. MedlinePlus advises immediate contact for these signs because a newborn cord infection can become serious quickly. Read its newborn umbilical cord warning signs. A webpage cannot assess an unwell newborn.

Usually not a worry

  • A dark, hard stump: brown, gray, or black usually means it is drying.
  • Hanging by a thread: leave it alone; pulling can restart bleeding.
  • A few drops of blood: a small spot around separation can be normal if it stops and dries.
  • A small scab or briefly raw center: this can appear as the navel closes.

Spreading redness, foul odor, pus, repeated bleeding, or a baby who seems unwell changes the advice—contact a clinician rather than giving the area more time.

Useful today

  1. At one diaper change, notice whether the stump is dry and whether the nearby skin has its usual color.
  2. Fold the clean diaper below the stump and choose loose clothing that does not rub it.
  3. Set up a sponge bath instead of putting baby in a tub; gently clean and dry the stump only if it gets soiled.
  4. If you call, describe the redness, odor, drainage, bleeding, temperature, feeding, and alertness; a photo in good light may help the care team.
