Tummy time and awake play

Tummy time can begin in the first days: place an awake baby on their tummy and stay close enough to watch and respond the whole time. Start with short, low-pressure tries; if baby gets sleepy, move them onto their back in a separate safe sleep space.

What it looks like right now

Choose a calm, alert moment after waking or a diaper change. On a clear, firm area of the floor, lay baby tummy-down on a thin blanket. Bring your face close, talk softly, and keep their nose and mouth clear. A newborn may rest one cheek down, tuck their arms and legs in, make small movements, or lift or turn their head only for a moment. That is still practice.

Most babies can start a day or two after birth. The NIH Safe to Sleep tummy-time guide recommends two or three short sessions of about 3–5 minutes each day, then gradually longer, more regular sessions. Those numbers are a direction, not a test: end sooner when baby needs a break and try again later.

Awake and watched, every time

Stay with baby and keep checking their face, breathing, and alertness. Tummy time is never a sleep position, even for a short nap. If baby dozes or you need to step away, place them on their back in their own firm, flat, empty sleep space.

What may change next

  • Over the next few days: baby may stay settled for a little longer, turn their head from one cheek to the other, or briefly lift it. Look for alert eyes, easy breathing, and small arm and leg movements—not a high push-up.
  • Over the coming weeks: short tries can become more frequent or longer. Baby may lift their head more often, turn it more easily, and watch your face or a simple object for longer before tiring.
  • By about 2 months: NIH says to work toward 15–30 minutes total each day, split into manageable sessions. Holding the head up while on the tummy and moving both arms and both legs are among the CDC’s 2-month movement milestones.

Milestone ranges are guides, and progress is not perfectly linear. One day may go smoothly and the next may be mostly feeding, sleeping, and very brief play.

Reassuring signs

  • Baby is awake enough to respond, their face stays uncovered, and breathing and color look usual.
  • They move both sides of the body, even if the movements are small or uneven from moment to moment.
  • They briefly turn or lift their head, or simply try, then rest with the head to one side.
  • They settle after being picked up when they have had enough.
  • Across days and weeks, you notice a little more head control, comfort, looking, or time before tiring.

No single session needs to show every sign. The trend across ordinary play matters more than one difficult try.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

  • Baby consistently keeps their head turned one way, seems uncomfortable turning the other way, or a flat area is developing on the head.
  • Tummy time remains very distressing despite shorter tries or chest and lap alternatives.
  • Baby was born early or has reflux disease, special health needs, or equipment; ask for an individualized positioning plan, as the American Academy of Pediatrics advises.

Get prompt medical advice

Call sooner if baby suddenly uses one arm or leg much less than the other, seems unusually stiff or floppy, has difficulty holding the head and neck as skills develop, or loses a movement they previously had. The AAP’s physical-development guidance recommends sharing these concerns with the pediatrician rather than waiting for the next milestone.

Get urgent help now

Stop and call emergency services if baby is unresponsive, is not breathing normally, is struggling for each breath, or has blue or gray lips or skin. Follow the dispatcher’s instructions. These are emergency signs in the AAP guide to calling emergency medical services.

Usually not a worry

  • Fussing at first: many babies do not enjoy the unfamiliar position. End the try if fussing builds, help baby settle, and try a shorter session later.
  • Only a few seconds: awake play can be broken into tiny tries. Gradual practice matters more than finishing a timer.
  • Resting instead of lifting: newborn head control is limited. Resting with the face clear is expected; the 2-month skill is a later guide, not a newborn requirement.
  • An umbilical cord stump: tummy-to-tummy on an awake, reclined caregiver’s chest can be used before the stump falls off. Keep one hand on baby, keep the face visible, and do not let either person fall asleep.

Useful today

  1. Pick one alert window after waking or a diaper change; skip it when baby is hungry, very upset, or sleepy.
  2. Start on a clear floor. Stay within reach, get face-to-face, and stop before baby is exhausted.
  3. If floor time is uncomfortable, try baby tummy-down across your lap with head support or chest-to-chest while you are fully awake and stable.
  4. Use your voice and face as the toy. Awake play can also be cuddling, talking, singing, and letting baby look at you while supported.
  5. Notice whether baby turns toward both sides and moves both arms and legs over several sessions; share a persistent difference with the pediatrician.
