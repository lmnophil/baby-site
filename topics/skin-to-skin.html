Comfort & connection

Skin-to-skin

Skin-to-skin means holding a diaper-only baby chest-to-chest against an awake caregiver’s bare skin. It can support warmth, feeding, and calm in the first hours and remain useful in the days and weeks ahead—but the caregiver must stay alert, keep baby’s face and airway visible, and move baby to a separate safe sleep space before dozing.

What it can look like right now

Settle into a stable, semi-reclined position. Place baby upright and belly-down on your bare chest, with their shoulders and chest facing you. Turn the head to one side; keep the neck straight, the chin off the chest, and the nose and mouth uncovered. A blanket can go across baby’s back, away from the face. These are the American Academy of Pediatrics’ safe-positioning elements for skin-to-skin care.

Baby may become quiet, open their eyes, make small head and hand movements, root, nuzzle, or feed. Some babies drift to sleep. Keep watching the whole baby—face, breathing, color, movement, and warmth—rather than assuming quiet always means settled.

For medically stable newborns and caregivers, skin-to-skin after birth helps maintain body temperature, stabilize glucose, and begin breastfeeding, according to the CDC’s current maternity-care guidance. A non-birthing parent or other caregiver can provide it too, and it is useful whether baby feeds at the breast or by bottle.

An awake hold, not a sleep setup

Stay awake and able to respond throughout. If you feel sleepy, unsteady, distracted, or affected by medication, ask an alert adult for help or put baby on their back in a separate bassinet, crib, or play yard with a firm, flat, level surface and only a fitted sheet. Adult beds, couches, and armchairs are not safe places for baby to sleep; see the NIH Safe to Sleep guidance.

How it may change

  • In the first hours after birth: when caregiver and baby are stable, maternity guidance favors uninterrupted contact for at least the first hour and through the first feed. Baby may rest before rooting or attaching; that pause does not mean they are uninterested.
  • In the first days: try skin-to-skin when baby is waking for a feed, searching with their mouth, or unsettled. You may notice the body relax, hands open, or rooting begin. It can help with calm and feeding, but it does not replace feeding or a medical assessment.
  • Over the coming weeks: use it when it suits both of you—for connection, comfort, or feeding cues. There is no single home session length or finish date. A baby who missed immediate contact can still have it later.

The sequence and timing vary, and some babies need positioning help. The UNICEF UK Baby Friendly skin-to-skin guide describes these early behaviors and the need to keep checking breathing, color, tone, and temperature. Preterm, low-birth-weight, or medically unwell babies may need a specific skin-to-skin plan from their hospital team.

Reassuring signs

  • Baby’s face stays visible, with the head turned and the nose and mouth clear.
  • Breathing looks comfortable, without the chest pulling in, repeated grunting, or struggling for each breath.
  • Lips, tongue, face, and body keep their usual color in the room’s light.
  • Baby feels comfortably warm rather than sweaty or hot and has normal flexed tone and spontaneous movement.
  • Across repeated sessions, baby may settle, wake, root, or feed; no single response is required every time.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician or care team

  • You cannot find a position that keeps baby’s face visible and both bodies supported.
  • Baby was born early, has a medical condition or equipment, or you are unsure whether skin-to-skin is appropriate right now.

Get medical care now

  • Go to an emergency department now if baby is hard to wake.
  • Call the pediatrician or seek care now if a newborn looks or acts unusual, has a poor suck, or seems much less responsive than before.
  • Breathing becomes harder or baby repeatedly feels unusually cold or hot. Stop the session and seek advice; do not rely on skin-to-skin to correct a concerning change.

Get urgent help now

Call emergency services if baby cannot be woken, is not moving or is very weak, struggles for each breath, makes a new grunting sound with every breath, or has blue or gray lips, tongue, or face. Follow the dispatcher’s instructions. These thresholds come from the American Academy of Pediatrics’ newborn behavior symptom checker.

Usually not a worry

  • Brief or long sessions: at home, there is no quota. Stop when either of you needs a break.
  • A slow start: one baby may root quickly while another rests first. Get feeding help if baby also has a weak suck, repeatedly misses feeds, or is hard to wake.
  • Not breastfeeding: skin-to-skin can still offer warmth, calm, and connection.
  • Not enjoying it every time: closeness can also come from an alert caregiver holding a dressed baby. Do not force a session that feels unsafe or distressing.

Useful today

  1. Prepare baby’s separate safe sleep space before beginning.
  2. Choose a time when you are fully awake; have another alert adult nearby if you are exhausted, sore, or taking medicine that can make you drowsy.
  3. Use the quick position check: face visible, head turned, neck straight, nose and mouth clear, back supported and covered.
  4. Look at breathing, color, tone, and warmth throughout—not only at the start.
  5. Move baby to their own sleep space as soon as you feel sleepy.
