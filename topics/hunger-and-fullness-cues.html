Feeding

Hunger and fullness cues

Offer the breast or bottle when early hunger cues appear, then pause or stop when baby shows they are full. Crying is a late cue, and a very sleepy newborn may still need to be woken for feeds according to their clinician’s plan.

What the cues look like right now

Newborn cues are small movements, not a perfect schedule. Look at what baby’s mouth, head, hands, and sucking are doing together.

Early hunger cues

  • Bringing hands to the mouth repeatedly or keeping hands clenched.
  • Turning the head and opening the mouth to search for a breast or bottle. This is rooting.
  • Puckering, smacking, or licking the lips.
  • Becoming more active or fussy.

Crying often comes later and can make it harder for baby to settle and eat. The CDC’s hunger and fullness guide recommends offering a feed while baby is still calm.

Fullness cues

  • Slowing or stopping sucking.
  • Closing the mouth or releasing the nipple.
  • Turning the head away from the breast or bottle.
  • Opening and relaxing the hands and body.

Pause when these signs appear. If baby stays relaxed and does not resume hunger cues, end the feed; there is no need to coax baby to finish a bottle. If cues are unclear, pause briefly and offer again rather than pushing the nipple back into a closed mouth.

How the pattern may change

  1. In the first days: feeds are small and frequent, and some newborns are too sleepy to cue reliably. Babies getting breast milk may want to feed every 1–3 hours; the CDC describes about 8–12 breastfeeds in 24 hours. A clinician may recommend waking baby rather than waiting for a cue.
  2. Over the next few weeks: the same mouth, head, hand, and body cues often become easier to recognize. Feed length and spacing can still vary across the day, and feeding close together for a stretch—often called cluster feeding—can happen.
  3. Over the next few months: baby can gradually take more at a feed and may go longer between some feeds. Cues also change as baby becomes more alert and social, so keep watching behavior rather than expecting one fixed timetable.

These are broad patterns, not deadlines. See the CDC’s first-days-to-months feeding guidance. Premature babies and babies with jaundice, slow weight gain, or another medical concern may need a schedule from their pediatrician; follow that plan even when cues are quiet.

Signs feeding is going well

Look for a cluster of reassuring signs rather than treating any one cue as a pass-or-fail test:

  • Baby wakes or can be woken for feeds and has periods of alertness.
  • Sucking becomes rhythmic, with visible or audible swallowing.
  • Baby relaxes, releases, or seems content after at least some feeds.
  • Wet and dirty diapers follow the day-by-day target from the care team.
  • Weight and length track as expected when checked by a clinician.

Fullness behavior says that a feed may be over; it cannot prove how much milk baby received. The American Academy of Pediatrics feeding guide identifies diaper output and growth checks as useful indicators of adequate intake.

When to call or get help

Call the pediatrician

Ask during office hours if baby seems hungry after most feeds, feeding is repeatedly stressful, cues remain hard to interpret, or you need a clear plan for how often to wake baby. Feeding and diaper notes can make that conversation more useful.

Get prompt medical advice

Contact baby’s clinician now if a newborn takes very little, has fewer wet diapers than expected, cannot stay latched, has no strong suck or swallowing, becomes increasingly sleepy, or looks or acts abnormal. Do not wait for strong hunger cues if intake or alertness has changed.

Get urgent help now

Go to emergency care if baby is hard to wake or too weak to suck. Call 911 or the local emergency number if baby cannot be woken, is not moving, or is very weak. These are not normal fullness signs.

These escalation signs follow the American Academy of Pediatrics newborn-illness symptom checker. A webpage cannot assess a newborn who seems unwell.

Common variation that is usually okay

  • One feed may be short and focused while another includes pauses; the pattern across the day matters more than matching every feed.
  • Hands at the mouth or sucking after a feed can be self-soothing, and crying can mean a wet diaper, discomfort, tiredness, or a need for closeness—not only hunger.
  • Feeding more often at certain times can be normal when swallowing, diaper output, alertness, and growth remain reassuring.

The American Academy of Pediatrics guide to feeding patterns notes that babies vary in how often and how much they eat, and that sucking or crying does not always mean hunger. A sudden change paired with poor intake, low diaper output, or unusual sleepiness is different and deserves a call.

Useful today

  1. Watch for mouth, head, and hand cues before crying starts.
  2. If baby is already crying, calm them briefly, then offer the breast or bottle.
  3. During the feed, notice swallowing and pause when sucking slows or baby turns away.
  4. Do not require a bottle to be empty; offer more only if hunger cues return.
  5. Keep following the care team’s waking plan until they say cue-only feeding is appropriate.
