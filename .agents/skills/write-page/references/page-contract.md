# Baby topic page contract

Use this contract as an outcome checklist, not a rigid template. Choose headings that fit the topic and keep the page easy to scan.

## Required reader outcomes

### Orientation

- Open with one or two sentences that answer the page question directly.
- Establish the relevant age or time window without implying that every baby follows the same schedule.
- Include the most important safety qualification early when the topic carries immediate risk.

### Right now

- Describe concrete, observable behaviors, patterns, body signs, or setup details.
- Give useful ranges only when supported by a cited authority.
- Distinguish a recommendation from a description of common behavior.

### What changes next

- Use topic-appropriate time horizons, such as later today, over the next few days, in the coming weeks, or when a specific skill/setup transition appears.
- Pair each time range with a recognizable signal: what may start, stop, increase, decrease, or become more consistent.
- Explain what a caregiver can use to identify the transition.
- For milestones, say that ranges are guides and development is not perfectly linear.

Adapt the timeline to the subject:

- For chronological pages, use a compact sequence of time windows.
- For symptoms or recovery, explain the expected course and the signs that the course is no longer reassuring.
- For feeding or daily care, explain how cues, frequency, output, or technique may evolve.
- For fixed safety topics, explain when to reassess the setup as the baby grows or gains a new ability.

### Reassuring signs

- Describe what “going well” looks like in observable terms.
- Prefer trends and clusters of signs over a single pass/fail measure.
- State what can reasonably be checked at home without special equipment.

### Concerning signs

- Use calm labels such as “Call the pediatrician,” “Get prompt medical advice,” and “Get urgent help now” instead of calling a baby or caregiver “bad.”
- Separate routine questions from prompt and emergency thresholds.
- Give specific signs or numbers only when they are verified and cited.
- Include emergency instructions only when relevant; do not add generic alarm text to every page.
- Do not imply that a webpage replaces a clinician or local emergency services.

### Usually not a worry

- Name common variations or temporary patterns that caregivers often over-interpret.
- Bound reassurance with exceptions: say which accompanying sign would change the advice.
- Avoid dismissive language such as “just,” “obviously,” or “nothing to worry about.”

### Useful today

- End the guidance with a short, realistic action list.
- Favor observation, setup checks, cue-following, note-taking for a clinician, or another low-burden step relevant to the topic.
- Do not prescribe treatment beyond the cited public guidance.

### Sources

- Cite important claims where they appear in the body.
- End with a short source list containing the organization and descriptive page title.
- Link to the specific guidance page, not merely an organization homepage.
- Include only sources that directly support material page content; omit unused, redundant, or merely background reading.
- Make the page's most consequential guidance traceable to the source responsible for that recommendation.

## Writing and presentation standards

- Aim for roughly 600–1,000 words unless the topic is safer and clearer at another length.
- Write for a tired caregiver: lead with the answer, use plain language, and keep paragraphs short.
- Define unfamiliar medical terms on first use.
- Use semantic `article`, `header`, `section`, heading, paragraph, and list elements.
- Keep one `h1`; use sequential heading levels and do not use headings only for visual styling.
- Do not encode meaning with color alone.
- Make source links understandable out of context and indicate external resources naturally in their text.
- Avoid tables when a short list works better on a phone.

## Final content check

The finished page should let a caregiver answer:

- “What am I likely seeing now?”
- “What might change next, and how will I notice?”
- “What looks reassuring?”
- “What should make me call or act now?”
- “What variation can I usually give time?”
- “What can I do today?”
